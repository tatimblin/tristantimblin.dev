When people talk about machine learning and cars, they mean self-driving. That makes sense, since autonomy is one of the hardest problems in the field. But a car is also a sensor platform that rides along with you every day, and some of the best uses for that data are ones it was never built for.

Here's one. Ask older adults how often they drive, and their answers predict who will lose cognitive function faster. Your car doesn't need to ask.

## The research

Luke Miller, a friend of mine from LSU, [studied](https://repository.lsu.edu/gradschool_theses/5644/) 1,426 older adults from the Rush Memory and Aging Project, who took cognitive tests every year for an average of almost seven years. People who drove less had worse cognition at the start, and they declined faster from there. That held after accounting for depression, general mobility and demographics.

Driving frequency in that study came from a questionnaire. Luke's suggested next step was to look at how driving habits *change* over time, which is hard to do with a survey and trivial for a car.

Other researchers have started on the sensor side. A team at Washington University [put GPS loggers](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8204509/) in the cars of 139 cognitively normal older adults for a year. From driving data alone, a model picked out the ones with preclinical Alzheimer's disease (confirmed by spinal fluid biomarkers) with an F1 score of 0.82. Those are people with no symptoms yet.

A plug-in logger sees speed, distance and hard braking. A modern car sees far more, on every trip, without anyone installing anything.

## Why Fisker

I read Luke's work and wanted to build the real-time version. That's why I'm at Fisker.

We're early. The Ocean only started shipping this summer, on Magna's brand new EV platform. The obvious ML work is L3 driver assist, and that's where most of the attention goes. But the team is small enough that the ML direction isn't set, and the same signals that feed driver assist can feed other models too. I've written software for years and wanted to build something new that touches the physical world, and this is about as physical as it gets.

## What we're building

Every ML application on the car needs the same foundation: clean signals, at volume, ready to train on. So that's what we're building first.

- 2 million messages a second off the cars
- 8TB of signals a day, stored as parquet
- Signal data prepared for training

Once that exists, each new application is mostly a new model. One of our first finds the last 10 miles of range on the Ocean, enough to pass the Tesla Model Y. Nobody would call range an ML problem, and it's already paying off.

## What a health model needs

Detecting decline from driving is harder than detecting it in a study. A few things have to be true.

- **Passive.** The research uses surveys and add-on devices. The point of doing it in the car is that the driver does nothing.
- **A baseline per driver.** Decline is a change, not a number. A cautious driver isn't impaired, so the model has to compare you to yourself, which means months of history before it can say anything.
- **Knowing who's driving.** Shared cars mix two people's baselines. The car has to tell drivers apart before it can track one of them.
- **Consent first.** This is health data inferred from behavior. Drivers have to opt in, and they have to own what the car concludes about them.
- **Clinical validation.** The evidence so far is a few promising studies with hundreds of people, not thousands. A flag from your car should start a conversation with a doctor, never stand in for one.

None of these is a modeling problem. They're product and data problems, and the pipeline is where most of them get solved.

## Look past the obvious

Waymo, Zoox and Applied Intuition rightly dominate the conversation about ML and vehicles. But if you only treat the car as something that should drive itself, you leave most of its data on the table. Range, predictive maintenance and health all come from signals the car already has.

If you work on vehicle data, it's worth asking what else it can tell you, especially about the person driving. A car will know you better than almost any other device you own.
