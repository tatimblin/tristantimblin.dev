Most vehicle data gets read as a record of the car: battery, speed, location. But every trip is also a record of the person driving it: where they go, when, how fast they react. If you build a system that faithfully maps the physical world, you can test hypotheses later that nobody had when the data was collected. That's what drew me to automotive.

## Why Fisker

We're early. The Ocean only started shipping this summer, on Magna's brand new EV platform. Our obvious ML work is L3 driver assist, and that's where most of the attention goes. But the team is small enough that the ML direction isn't set, and the same signals that feed driver assist can feed other models too.

## The platform

Every ML application on the car needs the same foundation: clean signals, at volume, ready to train on. So that's what we're building first.

- Processing 2 million messages a second
- Storing 8TB a day
- Signal data prepared for training

Once that exists, each new application is mostly a new model. One of our first found the last 10 miles of range, taking the Ocean Extreme One to a 360-mile EPA rating, 30 more than the Tesla Model Y Long Range. Nobody would call range an ML problem, and it's already paying off.

## The research

That idea isn't mine. Luke Miller, a friend and PhD candidate at LSU, [studied](https://repository.lsu.edu/gradschool_theses/5644/) 1,426 older adults from the Rush Memory and Aging Project, who took cognitive tests every year for an average of almost seven years. People who drove less had worse cognition at the start, and they declined faster from there. That held after accounting for depression, general mobility and demographics.

Driving frequency in that study came from a questionnaire. Luke's suggested next step was to look at how driving habits *change* over time, which is hard to do with a survey and trivial for a car.

Other researchers have started on the sensor side. A team at Washington University [put GPS loggers](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8204509/) in the cars of 139 cognitively normal older adults for a year. From driving data alone, a model picked out the ones with preclinical Alzheimer's disease (confirmed by spinal fluid biomarkers) with an F1 score of 0.82. Those are people with no symptoms yet.

A plug-in logger sees speed, distance and hard braking. A modern car sees far more, on every trip, without anyone installing anything: reaction times, like how long it takes to brake when traffic stops ahead, and steering stability, like how steadily you hold your lane. I read Luke's work and wanted to build the real-time version. That's the model I came to Fisker for.

## How a health model could work

We haven't built this yet, but here's how I'd sketch it on the platform we have.

It starts with three kinds of features, all from signals the car already logs on every trip:

- **Habits**: how often you drive, how far, what time of day, and whether you stick to familiar routes. This is the survey question from Luke's study, measured instead of asked.
- **Reaction time**: when the radar sees the car ahead slow down, how long until your foot hits the brake.
- **Steering stability**: how much you drift within the lane, and how many small corrections it takes to hold it.

Then the model needs four things to turn those into something useful:

1. **Context.** Slow reactions on an icy night aren't decline. Each trip gets normalized for traffic, weather, road type and time of day before it counts.
2. **A baseline per driver.** A cautious driver isn't impaired, so the model compares you to yourself. That takes months of history, and it takes knowing who's driving, since a shared car mixes two people's baselines.
3. **Watch for drift.** One bad trip means nothing. What matters is a slow, sustained shift across several features over weeks or months, which is a change-detection problem more than a classification one.
4. **A gentle output.** Drivers opt in, and they own what the car concludes about them. A flag should suggest a conversation with a doctor, never stand in for one.

The hard part isn't the model, it's the labels. To know a drift means decline, you need drivers whose cognition is measured over time, like the people in Luke's study. Validating this means partnering with researchers who already follow them, and so far the evidence is a few promising studies with hundreds of people, not thousands.

## Look past the obvious

Self-driving rightly dominates the conversation about ML and vehicles, but it isn't the only thing the data is good for. If you work on vehicle data, it's worth asking what else it can tell you, especially about the person driving. A car will know you better than almost any other device you own.
